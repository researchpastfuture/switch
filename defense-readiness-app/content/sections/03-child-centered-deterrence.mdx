## Overview

This section develops what the authors call **child-centered deterrence**. The argument is that deterrence — usually discussed in terms of armies and arsenals — should be reframed around the protection of children, which the document treats as the organizing priority of the entire doctrine.

### Reframing deterrence

Conventional deterrence aims to make aggression too costly to attempt. The authors apply the same logic at the level of community and family: they argue that exploitation, trafficking, and predatory influence are deterred when protection is visible, coordinated, and locally rooted. In their framing, "the forgotten war at home" is the everyday work of making children harder to target and communities quicker to respond.

The document is careful to locate this work within lawful structures. It emphasizes coordination with schools, established reporting channels, and law enforcement, and it presents the family and the neighborhood as the first layers of awareness rather than as substitutes for proper authorities.

### A self-assessment, not a mandate

The interactive **Community Preparedness Self-Assessment** turns the section's themes into a practical checklist. Each item reflects a protective practice the document discusses, and checking it contributes to a readiness score that maps to a band — from "Getting started" to "Highly prepared." The items include:

- A discussed family safety and communication plan.
- Knowing the school's protection and reporting points of contact.
- Household online-use rules and parental controls.
- Connection to a neighborhood or community watch network.
- Knowing how to report exploitation or trafficking concerns.
- Children being able to name several trusted adults.
- Awareness of local counseling and recovery resources.

The tool is designed as a private reflection aid: nothing is submitted anywhere, and the score is meant to help a household decide which gaps to close next rather than to rank or judge.

### What the document emphasizes

Three points recur in this section. First, **prevention is relational** — the authors argue that trusted adults, open communication, and strong family routines do more to protect children than any single technical measure. Second, **reporting matters** — the document places weight on knowing, in advance, how and to whom to report concerns. Third, **recovery is part of protection** — awareness of counseling and recovery resources is treated as essential, not optional, because the authors view aftercare as integral to deterrence.

The section closes by framing preparedness as a shared civic habit: families that are prepared, the authors suggest, strengthen the families around them, and the most prepared households are encouraged to mentor others rather than treat readiness as a private achievement.
