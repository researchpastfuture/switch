## Overview

This section surveys what the document describes as the nation's threat posture across three categories — **border**, **cartel**, and **internal** — and presents them together on an interactive map so readers can see how the authors relate them.

### Three categories, one picture

The authors group the challenges they discuss into three overlapping types:

- **Border** concerns center on high-traffic crossing and smuggling corridors.
- **Cartel** concerns cover organized trafficking and distribution networks, including maritime and overland routes, regional distribution hubs, and ports of entry.
- **Internal** concerns address domestic vulnerabilities the document raises, such as online recruitment and exploitation and the transit chokepoints that connect regions.

The document's argument is that these are not isolated problems. The authors contend that the same routes and networks can carry different harms, and that treating border, cartel, and internal issues as a single connected posture gives a more accurate picture than addressing each in isolation.

### Reading the Threat Posture Map

The **Threat Posture Map** places illustrative markers on a simplified map of the United States. Each marker carries a category, a severity rating (1–3), and a short description, and readers can filter by category to focus on one type at a time. Marker color indicates category and size indicates severity.

Two cautions are worth stating, in keeping with the document's own framing. First, the markers are **illustrative and editable** — they represent the kinds of concerns the authors discuss and approximate locations, not a live intelligence feed or official threat assessment. Second, the map is a **conceptual aid**: it is meant to help readers understand how the categories relate geographically, not to direct any operational activity.

### What the document emphasizes

The authors use this section to make a case for **coordination**. They argue that because the three categories overlap, responses that involve federal, state, local, and community partners — operating lawfully and in cooperation with established authorities — are more effective than fragmented efforts. The map is paired later in the document with the rescue-operations protocol (Section 8) and the sample-legislation tools (Section 11), reflecting the authors' view that awareness must connect to lawful response and durable policy.

As elsewhere, this edition presents the threat posture as the authors describe it. Readers are encouraged to weigh the categories, severities, and relationships critically and to consult authoritative sources for current, verified information.
